📖 Grapheme–colour synesthesia trainer

Every letter and digit gets a colour (its identity) and a shape (its type). The goal is to move from conscious decoding — "square, red, that must be A" — to automatic recognition, where the colour arrives before the reasoning does.

🧠 What the research actually says
Bor, Rothen, Schwartzman, Clayton & Seth (2014), Scientific Reports, trained 14 adults on letter–colour associations for 9 weeks. The trained group showed synesthesia-like Stroop interference, and a mean IQ gain of about 12 points versus controls.

Read it with care: n = 14 trained participants, and the authors note the IQ result was not their primary outcome and needs replication. The synesthetic effects faded within three months of stopping. The reliable, repeatedly-measured finding is the one this app can actually check for you — the associations become automatic, which is what the Automaticity check measures.

🎮 Modes

    🔎 Pop-out search — the second automaticity measure

    The Stroop asks whether the colour intrudes when you want to ignore it. Pop-out asks the opposite question: whether the colour helps when you want to use it. A target is hidden in a field of 6, 12 or 20 symbols, and the app records how long you take at each size.

    If the association has become automatic, the target pops out and your time barely changes as the field grows — parallel search. If you are still decoding one symbol at a time, time climbs with set size. The fitted slope in milliseconds per item is the measurement, and it is reported in Progress. A slope near zero is the result worth chasing.

    🎧 Tone ID, and how a symbol sounds

    Until recently every symbol sat somewhere on one continuous pitch scale — 110 Hz up to 6.2 kHz, a whole tone apart, with nothing but frequency separating them. Naming a tone out of that set is an absolute pitch task. Absolute pitch is rare, and without it people can label only a handful of steps on a single continuous dimension; thirty-six was never learnable, and the ten digits sat high enough to be shrill as well. If that mode felt impossible, it was.

    The sound is now built the same way the picture is. A symbol's picture uses shape for the family and colour for the identity; its sound uses timbre for the family and pitch for the position inside it. Six timbres × six pitches covers all 36, and no single dimension ever asks you to split more than six ways.

    • Pure — smooth and round, like a flute · A–F
    • Reed — hollow and woody, like a clarinet · G–L
    • Bright — buzzy and edged, like brass · M–R
    • Bell — struck metal, ringing out · S–X
    • Pluck — short and dry, a plucked string · Y, Z, 0–3
    • Air — breathy and drifting, two tones beating · 4–9

    The six pitches are one octave of a major pentatonic — C, D, E, G, A, C — so the steps are wide, musical, and in the range where pitch is easiest to hear. Every family uses the same six notes. What changes between families is the character of the sound, which is a categorical judgement rather than a fine measurement, and far easier to make.

    It starts small. Tone ID opens at stage 1: one timbre, six pitches. Eight correct in a row adds the next family, and three misses hands one back. A wrong answer plays the right sound and then the one you picked, because what teaches you is the difference between them, not being told which was correct.

    ⚖️ Match check

    Symbols on top, a spelling underneath: same or different? Two keys, no typing, so it isolates reading speed from motor speed. When the two differ, the substituted letter is drawn from your own confusion pairs wherever there is data — so the decision is hard for exactly the reason the training cares about.

    🎯 How the scheduler picks symbols

    Symbols are not drawn uniformly. Each one carries a priority built from four things:

    • Accuracy — the more often you miss it, the more often it returns.
    • Time since you last saw it — untouched symbols resurface (spacing effect).
    • Response speed — a symbol you get right but slowly is not yet automatic, so it keeps its priority.
    • Novelty — anything never seen goes to the front of the queue.

    This priority is saved between sessions, so closing the tab no longer resets what the app knows about you.

    ⏱️ Adaptive timer

    Two separate questions, handled separately.

    How long a question deserves. Decoding effort scales with the number of distinct symbols you have to look up, not with word length. sees is two lookups, not four — once you have decoded the first s the second is free. So the window is base + unit × demand, where demand counts unique symbols, each weighted by how well you know it (never seen ×1.35, mastered ×0.85). base is fixed overhead — reading the prompt, typing the answer.

    How hard it should be. A weighted staircase (Kaernbach 1991) moves unit until you sit at the target accuracy, 85% by default. For a staircase to settle at accuracy P, the steps must satisfy down / up = (1 − P) / P — so a correct answer shrinks unit by about 1.7% and a miss grows it by 10%.

    What changed from 2.4.2: the old rule shrank the window 10% on a correct answer and grew it 10% on a miss. Equal steps settle wherever wins and losses balance — for that pair, 47.5% accuracy. That is why the clock always ran away to something unplayable. It also reset to the full starting time every session, discarding the calibration each time.
    • It is remembered. unit is saved per mode and reloaded next time, with a 15% warm-up allowance on the first question of a session.
    • Repeated letters are free. level (3 unique) gets the same window as seed (3 unique), despite being longer.
    • Weak letters buy time. A word made of symbols you keep missing earns more room than its length suggests; one made of mastered symbols earns less.
    • The live Window readout in the drill header shows the current question's window.
    • Change the target in Tools → Preferences → Difficulty target, or switch the clock off with Timed drills. Speed is still recorded either way.

    📚 Where the words come from

    Word and Flash draw from about 6,800 common English words (3–7 letters), every one checked against a system dictionary and filtered against a common-usage list, so no abaft or zyzzyva. Words are drawn without replacement — the bag empties before anything repeats — and word length ramps up as your streak grows.

    Phrases are not a list at all. They are generated from a small grammar of templates and word banks, which yields tens of thousands of distinct, grammatical sentences. The 22 fixed phrases of earlier versions are gone; the originals you liked survive as idioms in the mix.

    🌡️ The automaticity check (Stroop)

    A letter is printed in a colour that either matches its trained colour or clashes with it. You name the ink colour and ignore the letter. If the association has become automatic you cannot ignore it — clashing trials get slower. That gap, in milliseconds, is your interference score, and watching it grow is the most honest evidence that the training is doing something.

    Give it at least ~30 trials before reading anything into the number. Below that it is mostly noise.

    💡 Practical advice

    • Short and daily beats long and occasional. Ten minutes a day is the whole method.
    • Start in Learn, then Decode, and only add Word/Phrase once single symbols feel effortless.
    • When you keep missing a pair, go to Confusions — mixed-up pairs need contrast, not more volume.
    • Export your progress occasionally. Local storage is not a backup.
